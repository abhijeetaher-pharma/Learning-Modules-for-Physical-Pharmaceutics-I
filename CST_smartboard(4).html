🌡️ Critical Solution Temperature
Partially Miscible Liquids & Critical Solution Temperature
Some liquids mix completely only beyond a certain temperature — that critical temperature is the CST or Consolute Temperature
💧
Partially Miscible Liquids
Liquids that dissolve in each other only to a limited extent at a given temperature. Two separate liquid layers (conjugate solutions) form — each saturated with the other component.
When phenol and water are mixed: two layers form — (1) phenol dissolved in water (lower layer) and (2) water dissolved in phenol (upper layer). These two coexisting saturated solutions are called conjugate solutions.
🌡️
Critical Solution Temperature (CST)
The temperature at which complete miscibility is reached — the two conjugate liquid layers merge into a single homogeneous phase. Also called Consolute Temperature.
Above UCST or below LCST, the mixture is completely miscible in all proportions — single phase. Between UCST and LCST (in closed-loop systems), the mixture is only partially miscible — two phases.
⬆️
Upper CST (UCST)
Miscibility increases with temperature. Above UCST: completely miscible (one phase). Below UCST: two layers. Most common type.
Example: Phenol–Water (UCST = 68.1°C at 36.1% phenol)
Other examples of UCST systems:
Methanol–Cyclohexane: 49°C (29% methanol)
Aniline–Water: 59.6°C (52% aniline)
Carbon disulphide–Methanol: 49.5°C
⬇️
Lower CST (LCST)
Miscibility decreases with temperature. Below LCST: completely miscible. Above LCST: two separate layers form — unusual behaviour!
Example: Triethylamine–Water (LCST = 18.5°C)
Also: Nicotine–Water system has BOTH UCST (208°C) and LCST (61°C) — partially miscible over 61–208°C range. Forms a closed-loop solubility curve.
UCST: miscible above Tc    |    LCST: miscible below Tc
At CST: the two conjugate layers merge → one homogeneous phase  ·  This temperature is the Consolute Temperature
⛽
Analogy — Oil & Water with Heat
Think of oil and water in a bottle. Normally two layers — no matter how hard you shake. Now imagine a magic temperature where they suddenly become one clear liquid. That magic temperature is the CST! Below it — two layers. Above it — one homogeneous liquid. In the phenol–water system, 68.1°C is that magic temperature.

Nicotine–Water System
LCST = 61°C (below this: fully miscible)
UCST = 208°C (above this: fully miscible)
61–208°C: partially miscible (two phases)

This creates a closed-loop solubility envelope. The system is miscible at extremes of temperature, but separates in the middle range.
Why the Closed Loop?
At low T: hydrogen bonding between nicotine N and water → miscible
At intermediate T: H-bonds break → two phases
At very high T: thermal energy overcomes all interactions → miscible again (like UCST)

⚗️ Effect of Impurities on CST
How Impurities Change the CST
Addition of a third substance (impurity) to a partially miscible system changes the CST — use this to determine purity of substances

⬆️
Raises UCST
Third substance dissolves preferentially in ONE phase → reduces mutual solubility → UCST rises
Example: 0.1M naphthalene added to phenol-water → dissolves only in phenol layer → UCST rises by ~20°C
Potassium iodide dissolved in phenol raises UCST of phenol-water system
⬇️
Lowers UCST
Third substance dissolves equally in BOTH phases → increases mutual solubility → UCST falls
Example: Sodium oleate reduces CST of phenol-water → phenolic disinfectants (Lysol) made miscible with water
NaCl in small amounts can also lower UCST of some systems
📐
CST as Purity Test
Since impurities change CST, a pure phenol-water mixture will always have UCST at exactly 68.1°C. Adulterated phenol gives a different CST value → used as a purity criterion in BP/IP.
💊 Pharmaceutical Applications
Why CST Matters in Pharmacy
Critical solution temperature has direct applications in formulation, quality control, extraction, and drug delivery. Click each card.
🧴
1. Phenolic Disinfectants (Lysol)
Phenol is partially miscible with water (UCST 68.1°C). Adding sodium oleate LOWERS the CST below room temperature → phenol becomes completely miscible with water → Lysol / cresol soap solution is made.
Cresol (pKa similar to phenol) + sodium oleate soap → CST lowered well below room temperature → completely miscible disinfectant. The CST principle allows high-concentration phenolic antiseptics to be formulated as clear aqueous solutions.
⚗️
2. Purity Testing of Substances
Pure phenol has UCST exactly 68.1°C with water. Impure phenol gives a different CST. CST determination is used as a purity criterion in pharmacopoeias (IP, BP) to detect adulteration.
Official IP test for phenol purity: determine CST with water. Any deviation from 68.1°C indicates the presence of impurities (other phenol derivatives, moisture, other organics). CST is more sensitive than most chemical purity tests.
🧪
3. Salting-out & Extraction
Adding a third substance to a partially miscible system to increase miscibility is called blending. Used in pharmaceutical extraction to select best solvent for drug isolation from plant material.
When a foreign substance lowers the UCST below room temperature, the two liquids become completely miscible → useful for extracting drugs with mixed solvents. Conversely, raising CST can be used to SEPARATE phases (salting-out technique in API purification).
🚀
4. Smart Drug Delivery (LCST Polymers)
PNIPAM (poly-N-isopropylacrylamide) shows LCST at ~32°C (body temp). Below 32°C: polymer dissolves in water (drug loaded). Above 32°C: polymer collapses → triggered drug release at body temperature!
Thermosensitive drug delivery systems based on LCST:
• Inject cool solution (miscible) at body temperature polymer collapses → drug released at target site
• Used for cancer drug delivery to tumour sites (slightly higher temperature)
• Ophthalmic thermogelling formulations (solution at room T, gel at body T)
🧀
5. Polymer Characterisation
UCST and LCST of polymer solutions help characterise polymer molecular weight, architecture, and compatibility. Used in designing controlled release polymer matrices and coating materials.
UCST polymers swell at high temperature → used in hot-melt extrusion for amorphous solid dispersions.
LCST polymers used as thermogelling bases for:
• Sustained release injections (cold = liquid, body temp = gel)
• Rectal/vaginal drug delivery (sol-gel transition)
🌡️
6. Formulation Stability
Partially miscible excipient systems in pharmaceutical formulations must stay in the correct phase state during storage. Understanding CST ensures formulations do not phase-separate on storage or during use.
Phenol injection BP: phenol (partially miscible with water) → formulated at concentration and temperature above/below CST to remain as a single phase → stable, homogeneous injection. Phase separation would give inconsistent dosing and product failure.
⚡
CST Links Theory to Practice
From phenolic antiseptics to smart cancer drug delivery, CST is a fundamental thermodynamic concept that determines whether two pharmaceutical liquids mix or separate. Every pharmacist needs to understand it to formulate stable, effective liquid preparations.

Slide Navigation
Step 1 — Hook ⏱ 3 min
"Add a drop of phenol to water. Two layers form! Now I heat it to 70°C — suddenly ONE clear liquid. Cool it back — two layers return. What is happening?" This is CST in action.
🗣 Say: "The temperature at which those two layers become ONE is the Critical Solution Temperature. Today we learn to draw, calculate, and use this concept."
Step 2 — UCST ⏱ 6 min
Show the Phenol-Water interactive diagram. Drag slider from 0 to 80°C. Watch conjugate compositions converge at 68.1°C consolute point. Mark tie lines with the arrow annotation tool.
👥 Ask: "At 40°C, read off the two conjugate compositions from the tie line. What % phenol is in each layer?"
Step 3 — LCST ⏱ 5 min
Show triethylamine-water. Start at 5°C (one clear phase). Drag slider UP to 25°C — two layers suddenly appear. "This is the OPPOSITE of what we just saw. WHY?" Explain H-bond breaking.
Key concept: At low T, H-bonds between N of Et₃N and water keep them mixed. Heating breaks H-bonds → phase separation. This is LCST.
Step 4 — Both CST ⏱ 4 min
Show nicotine-water closed loop. Drag slider from 0 to 250°C. Below 61°C: one phase. 61-208°C: two phases. Above 208°C: one phase again. "What shape is this curve?" — Closed loop / ellipse.
👥 Ask: "Why are there TWO critical temperatures here?" Answer: H-bond effect at low T (LCST) + thermal energy effect at high T (UCST).
Step 5 — Impurities ⏱ 5 min
Show impurity effect. Add naphthalene (raises CST) vs sodium oleate (lowers CST). Connect to Lysol formulation. Emphasise: CST as purity test — deviation from 68.1°C means impure phenol.
Application: "A pharmacist measures CST of a phenol sample and gets 72°C instead of 68.1°C. What does this mean?" (Impurity present that raises CST — contaminated phenol)
Step 6 — Assess ⏱ 10 min
Run Poll (3 questions). Then Explorer — ask students to click a point in two-phase region and predict what they see in a beaker. Finally launch fullscreen Quiz.
