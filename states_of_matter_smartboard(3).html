B. Pharmacy — Year 2  ·  Physical Pharmaceutics

The three states of matter

All matter exists in solid, liquid, or gas form — determined by the balance between intermolecular forces and kinetic energy.

Crystalline solid
Ordered lattice
Regular 3D repeating pattern. Sharp melting point. Most APIs — aspirin, paracetamol, ibuprofen — are crystalline.
Amorphous solid
Disordered structure
No long-range order. Melts over a range. Higher energy state → higher apparent solubility but lower stability.
Supercritical fluid
Beyond gas & liquid
Above critical T and P. CO₂-SCF used for API extraction and nanoparticle formation in pharma.

Changes of state — phase transitions

Matter transitions between states when temperature or pressure changes. Each transition involves latent heat at a constant temperature.

0% Solid
Latent heat
Why temperature stays constant
During a phase change, energy breaks or forms intermolecular bonds rather than increasing kinetic energy — so temperature holds steady until the transition is complete.
Lfusion < LvaporisationMore energy is needed to fully separate particles than to loosen them.
Vapour pressure
Tendency to vaporise
Pressure exerted by vapour in equilibrium with its liquid. Increases with temperature. Boiling = vapour pressure equals atmospheric pressure. Critical for inhaler design, packaging, and drug stability.

Phase diagram (P–T diagram)

Maps the state of a substance at any combination of temperature and pressure. Three landmarks define the diagram.

Triple point
Three phases coexist
Unique T and P where all three states are in equilibrium. Water: 0.01°C, 611.7 Pa. Defines the kelvin temperature scale.
Critical point
Supercritical fluid zone
Above critical T and P, liquid and gas are indistinguishable. Supercritical CO₂ (31.1°C, 73.8 bar) is used for drug extraction and nanoparticle synthesis.
Sublimation curve
Foundation of lyophilisation
Below the triple point, solids vaporise directly. Freeze-drying (lyophilisation) exploits this — ice sublimes under vacuum preserving thermolabile biologics.

Polymorphism — exam focus
Same API, different crystal forms
An API can exist in multiple crystalline arrangements (polymorphs) with different melting points, solubility, and stability. The Ritonavir case (1998): Form II appeared spontaneously with far lower bioavailability — causing a global drug recall. ICH Q6A mandates polymorph characterisation during drug development.
Lyophilisation process
Four stages
1. Freezing — cool to −40 to −80°C; ice crystals form.

2. Primary drying — apply vacuum below triple point; ice sublimes (removes ~95% water).

3. Secondary drying — raise T; bound water desorbs.

4. Sealing — under nitrogen or vacuum; reconstitutable cake results.
Amorphous vs crystalline — key exam pointAmorphous solids have higher free energy → higher apparent solubility (useful for BCS Class II drugs) but lower physical stability (risk of recrystallisation during storage). Stabilised in ASDs using polymers like HPMC-AS or PVP-VA.
Remember for vivaVapour pressure, surface tension, and viscosity are all temperature-dependent liquid properties that directly influence dissolution testing, filling operations, and inhalation formulation performance.

Quick quiz

Tap a question to reveal the answer. Suitable for self-test or classroom discussion.

Which state has definite volume but no definite shape? ›
Liquid. Particles are close enough for a fixed volume, but free to flow — so the liquid takes the shape of its container. Pharmaceutical examples: syrups, injections, suspensions, emulsions.
What is sublimation? Give a pharmaceutical example. ›
Sublimation is direct solid → gas transition without passing through the liquid state. It occurs below the triple point pressure. Pharmaceutical example: camphor and iodine sublime at room temperature; lyophilisation exploits ice sublimation under vacuum to dry thermolabile biologics without exposing them to liquid water.
Why does temperature stay constant during a phase change? ›
During a phase change, energy (latent heat) is used entirely to break or form intermolecular bonds rather than increasing particle kinetic energy. Since temperature measures average kinetic energy, it stays constant until the transition is complete — seen as a flat plateau on the heating curve.
State the triple point of water and its significance in lyophilisation. ›
The triple point of water is 0.01°C at 611.7 Pa — the only condition where ice, water, and vapour coexist in equilibrium. In lyophilisation, chamber pressure is maintained below this value (typically 100–200 mTorr) so that ice sublimes directly without melting, preserving protein structure and drug activity.
What is polymorphism and why does it matter in drug development? ›
Polymorphism is the ability of a solid to exist in more than one crystal lattice form. Polymorphs have different melting points, solubility, dissolution rate, and bioavailability. ICH Q6A requires identification and control of polymorphic forms. Classic example: ritonavir Form II spontaneously appeared post-launch with much lower bioavailability, forcing product withdrawal.
Compare amorphous and crystalline solids for solubility and stability. ›
Crystalline: ordered lattice, defined melting point, lower free energy, lower solubility, higher physical stability. Amorphous: disordered structure, higher free energy, higher apparent solubility (beneficial for BCS Class II drugs), but thermodynamically unstable — prone to recrystallisation during storage. Polymers (HPMC-AS, PVP-VA) in ASDs inhibit recrystallisation.
What happens above the critical point of a substance? ›
Above critical temperature and pressure, the substance becomes a supercritical fluid — intermediate between liquid (high density) and gas (high diffusivity, low viscosity). Supercritical CO₂ (Tc 31.1°C, Pc 73.8 bar) is used in pharmaceutical manufacturing for API extraction, purification, and nanoparticle production via RESS or SAS processes.
Explain the four stages of lyophilisation in order. ›
(1) Freezing: product is cooled to −40 to −80°C; ice crystals form and solutes concentrate. (2) Primary drying: chamber pressure is reduced below the triple point; ice sublimes removing ~95% of water. (3) Secondary drying: shelf temperature is raised to desorb bound water. (4) Sealing: vials are sealed under nitrogen or vacuum to prevent moisture uptake. The resulting lyophilised cake is easily reconstituted and has an extended shelf life.
